I made a drawing based on an episode of Ghost in the Shell. 

## Ghost in the Shell

Ghost in the Shell is a Japanese anime series about a team of cyborgs in a near-future (~2030), rather dystopian Japan. It is based on a manga by <span style="font-variant-caps: small-caps;">Masamuni</span> Shirō serialised between 1989 and 1991. I can’t say I like the manga but I to like the anime very much, despite what I consider its shortcomings. 

The reason why in English the series is called Ghost in the Shell is because of the concept of a "ghost" which is the remnant of the human body and brain in the shell of the cyberbody. In Japanese, the manga and anime series are called _kōkaku kidōtai_ (攻殻機動隊) which is usually translated as "Mobile Armoured Riot Police" but a more accurate translation is "Offensive Armoured Public Security Unit". A _kidōtai_ is actually a specific unit of the police in Japan, responsible for public security and disaster response, but they are defensive rather than offensive.

## Affection

There have been many incarnations of the anime. This article is about Episode 11, titled "Affection 草迷宮", of Ghost in the Shell Stand-Alone Complex 2nd Gig, a TV series which ran from 2002 to 2005. 

For the purpose of this article, it is enough to introduce three key characters: <span style="font-variant-caps: small-caps;">Kusanagi</span> Motoko (草薙素子), known as the Major, and her colleague Batō (バトー). They are both working for a specialised police unit called Public Security Section 9 (公安９課, _kōan kyūka_). The Major is a female-looking cyborg, Batō a male-looking one. In this episode they are testing the abilities of new recruits for Section 9.

## The story

A scene in the Japanese city of Newport (新浜市, _niihama-shi_). It is summer and very hot. The Major is seen leaving a water bus, and climbing up a set of stairs between two tall buildings. A small ball bounces down the stairs and she catches it in her left hand. She disappears into a maze of small streets and the recruits, whose task it is to trail here, lose track of her entirely.

But the Major herself is shifted into an alternate reality, with empty streets and recently abandoned shops and restaurants, food still on the plates, cigarettes still smoking. She suspects she's being hacked but continues and ends up in front of a shop with the sign "Memento Store" (牢記物店,_rōkimono-ten_). The shop stores items that held important memories for their depositors. 

She comes across a cyberbody which seems to be that of herself as a kid, and another, that of a boy of the same age, about eight or ten years old. They have been seated in an old car. The Major meets the shopkeeper, an elderly lady, and asks her about them. The shopkeeper would like to tell her the story but there is not enough time as the shop is about to close. The Major promises to return the next day. On leaving the shop her reality is restored: a kid bumps into here; it is evening, the streets are full of life and fireworks are erupting in the sky.

The next day she returns to the store and Batō is following her. She goes up the stairs leading to the warren of narrow streets bouncing the ball with her left hand, and when Batō reaches the top of the stairs, she is gone.

Back in the alternate reality, she enters the shop. The shopkeeper offers her tea and tells the sad story of the kids to whom the cyberbodies belonged. Both were the only survivors of an airplane crash. The girl was transplanted into a cyberbody as it was the only way to save her life. The boy was paralysed except for his left arm but he refused a cyberbody. While the girl was in a critical condition, the boy started folding _origami_ cranes (折り鶴, _oridzuru_) with only his left hand. Then one day the girl disappears, and the boy thinks she had died. In reality she is transferred to a different hospital to get her cyberbody and revalidation, but he does not know that. Pining for her, he keeps on folding cranes for the next two years. Then the girl, now in a cyberbody, reappears, but he does not recognise her. She tries to convince him to get a cyberbody too but he says “Only if you can fold _origami_ cranes with your left hand”. The girl tries and tries but she can't do it as her cyberbody is too crude. So in the end she leaves and they never see one another again, but after she's gone the boy realises who she was and finally accepts to be cyberised. 

## A thousand cranes

The folding of one thousand origami cranes (千羽鶴, _senbadzuru_) is a Japanese tradition derived from ancient folklore. In Japan, the crane is considered sacred and a symbol for longevity and good health. It is said to live for a thousand years, and therefore, folding a thousand cranes would grant a wish, in particular for health and recovery from illness.

In 1950, the _senbadzuru_ tradition was revived by <span style="font-variant-caps: small-caps;">Sasaki</span> Sadako, a girl who survived the atomic bombing of Hiroshima at the age of two. Ten years later she would finally die of leukemia caused by the radiation, but she managed to fold more than a thousand cranes before she died. Garlands of origami cranes are still a very common sight in Japan, especially at Shintō shrines. 

## What happened to the Major

There has been much discussion about this episode as being out of character with the series and for being implausible. It is one of my favourites. In fact, my only qualm with it is the car in the shop, which has no narrative role and does not makes any sense at all to me. The only explanation is that the director liked Mini Coopers.

One can wonder, if the Major's cyberbrain got hacked, and that of the recruits and that of Batō as well, who could have done this? It would have to be a super-hacker (超ウィザード級ハッカー, _chō WIZAADO kyū_ HAKKAA as it is called in the series). I see only two possibilities: either the Major did this herself, but subconsciously, triggered by the ball; or <span style="font-variant-caps: small-caps;">Kuze</span> Hideo  (the boy) did this, to show the Major that they know one another. But <span style="font-variant-caps: small-caps;">Kuze</span> only appears in the next episode of the series and even there he doesn't come into contact with the Major. I therefore lean towards the first explanation. Another reason for this is that the second time, the Major is bouncing the ball herself in her left hand, meaning that she has realised that this was the trigger the first time.

If the story happened in reality, then the question would be why the Major would need to go to a special shop to have a total stranger tell her the story of her own childhood. But if we assume that the ball triggered a dream-like flash back which appears like a hack because of the cyberbrain functionality, it all makes sense.

## The significance of the ball

The ball is a (手鞠,_temari_), a kind of ball traditionally made from pieces of old kimono cloth and used in a Japanese children’s handball game. In the episode, when the girl returns in her cyberbody to visit the boy, she is bouncing such a ball. She drops it and the boy catches it in his left hand.

The game is typically accompanied by a song, called _temari-uta_ (手鞠歌), and this is relevant as well because of the Japanese title of the episode, "草迷宮 affection". 草迷宮 (_kusameikyū_) here means "Kusanagi' Labyrinth", 草 (_kusa_) being the first character of her name. But the word _kusa_ means "grass" so it is also "The Grass Labyrinth", which is the title of a novel from 1908 by IZUMI Kyōka . In that novel, a man, <span style="font-variant-caps: small-caps;">Hagoshi</span> Akira, goes looking all over the country to remember a particular _temari-uta_ that his mother, who has died, used to sing to him. He ends up at a haunted villa in Akiya (秋谷, on the Miura peninsula; the name is a pun, as _akiya_ also means vacant house), where he finds a mysterious _temari_ and the whispers of a childhood friend, Ayame, who he assumed to have died. It's a story about memories and childhood friends and mysterious happenings, so the links with the episode are clear.

The part of the _temari-uta_ which Akira recalls is the epigraph of the novel:

I found a translation in a very old book (1901), ["A Japanese Miscellany ..." by the famous Lafcadio Hearn](https://archive.org/details/ajapanesemiscel01heargoog). It is a song from Shinano (信濃) province, an old province of Japan that is now Nagano Prefecture. Hearn lists it in his chapter "Narrative-songs" rather than "Battledoor-songs and Ball-songs", which makes more sense as it is clearly a story, whereas _temari-uta_ are usually counting songs.

> From the swamp beyond there rose up a serpent,<br>
> in the likeness of the youngest daughter of the wealthy Hachiman.<br>
> Well did it assume that form, skilfully standing.<br>
> Holding in its hands two gems,<br>
> and wearing upon its feet shoes of gold,<br>
> it traversed mountains and fields,<br>
> crying out the while:<br>
> — "Call there! — Call here!" <br>
> 
> Then did it meet  a grass-cutter, and say to him:<br>
> — "Fair husband, deign to give me a girdle"<br>
> — To give you both a girdle and a hat will be easily done,> if you become my wife. Then every morning, early rising, > I will arrange your hair." <br>
> — Wait then — patiently wait until the season of the blooming of flowers.<br>

("girdle" here is an _obi_, the belt of a _kimono_)

Hearn writes

> This is the story of a serpent assuming the shape of a certain man's daughter. Stories of serpent-women and dragon-women abound in Japanese literature. Probably both this song and the preceding one were inspired by the memory of some old romance or drama.

And he was right: "The youngest daughter of the wealthy Hachiman" (八幡長者乙娘,_hachiman chōjia no otomusume_), was a _kabuki_ play from 1715.

## The depiction of <span style="font-variant-caps: small-caps;">Kusanagi</span> Motoko 

What I don't like about the Ghost in the Shell anime, and even less about the manga, is the overly sexualised way in which the character of <span style="font-variant-caps: small-caps;">Kusanagi</span> Motoko is depicted, with overly large breasts and -- especially in the earlier series -- needlessly revealing clothing which draws attention to her breasts and hips. Of course the Major is a strong, independent and powerful female cyborg, so this look might be her personal statement, a bit like the look which [Naomi Wu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naomi_Wu) chose for herself. But based on the imagery in the manga, I don't think that is the case. I therefore much prefer her depiction in the latest instalment, ARISE.

## The issue of _mukokuseki_

Characters in manga and anime often don't look Japanese. The style is called _mukokuseki_ (無国籍) meaning "of no specific nationality" and works in the manga context but it raises the question of how to portray the Major in a more realistic style because representing her as a white person is not right, even though she has fair skin and blue eyes.

I have based my depiction of <span style="font-variant-caps: small-caps;">Kusanagi</span> Motoko  on a Japanese stage play from 2015, "Ghost is Alive", where the actress <span style="font-variant-caps: small-caps;">Aono</span> Kaede (青野楓) plays the role of the Major. Based on  pictures from that performance, the live actors are very close to their anime counterparts.
